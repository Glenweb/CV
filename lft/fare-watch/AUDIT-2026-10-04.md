> ## The 17 lesser issues — and the bigger thing they uncovered
>
> **Two of the 17 were my error.** British Airways and Turkish Airlines already held the
> correct published personal item in their bundled rows. The audit read a dump that filtered
> `__personal_item` rows, so I reported them missing. BA needed nothing; Turkish needed only
> its 4 kg limit recorded. The real count was 15.
>
> **The tool never received any of the corrections.** The checker carries its own copy of the
> data in a different shape (`maxCm`/`maxIn`, bundled personal item nested on its parent), and
> `airlines.json` was derived *from* it. Fixing the dataset changed nothing on the site:
> **42 rows had diverged** — all 15 measurement corrections plus the 17 lesser fixes. The
> consistency test passed throughout because it compared names, enforcement and prose counts,
> never dimensions. `sync-tool-from-dataset.py` now rewrites the tool from the dataset, and
> `test-consistency.py` fails on any dimension or linear-sum drift.
>
> **A pre-existing bug, worse than anything in the audit.** The size comparison ran in inches
> against `maxIn`, stored to one decimal place. A bag entered as exactly 56 cm became
> 22.047 in and failed a 22.0 in limit, so **every bag at exactly the published centimetre
> limit was told "too large by 0 cm", on every airline.** That is the most common case there
> is — a bag sold as "55 × 40 × 20" checked against a 55 × 40 × 20 limit. The comparison now
> runs in centimetres against `maxCm`, the authoritative field.
>
> **Linear-sum limits are now enforced.** Nine airlines cap the three dimensions added
> together. For JAL and ANA the cap binds beyond the per-axis limits: both allow
> 55 × 40 × 25 per axis but only 115 cm combined, so the tool was passing bags they refuse.
>
> Two self-inflicted regressions caught and fixed on the way: an `add` that duplicated an
> existing row code (Air Canada) and silently broke the revert, and a sync that overwrote
> Spirit's published `18 × 14 × 8 in` with the converted `17.7 × 13.8 × 7.9`.
>
> Suites: consistency 15, ceased 13, linear 14, embed 14 — all passing.

### The three worst

1. **Allegiant Air** — paid carry-on recorded as 56 × 36 × 23 against a published
   55 × 40 × 25. A 40 cm bag Allegiant accepts fails our 36 cm limit, so the reader pays
   to check a bag they did not need to.
2. **Finnair** — recorded 56 × 45 × 25 against a published 55 × 40 × 23. This one errs the
   other way: we tell the reader a bag passes, Finnair charges €60 to put it in the hold.
3. **Singapore Airlines** — we invented a box. SQ publishes only a 115 cm linear-sum limit;
   our 56 × 40 × 20 sums to **116**, so a bag passing our checker at those exact maxima
   breaks SQ's actual rule.

### One cause behind five of them

`25 × 33 × 20` is flydubai's published personal item. It also sits in the dataset as the
personal item for **Aer Lingus** and **Norwegian** — neither of which publishes it — and it
is the only triple in the dataset not in descending order. Separately, `40 × 30 × 10`
appears as the personal item for **Lufthansa, SWISS and Austrian**, all Lufthansa Group;
all three publish 40 × 30 × 15.

Both look like copy-paste during data entry, and the pattern predicted the SWISS and
Austrian defects before I checked them.

## 17 lesser issues

Mostly **missing personal-item rows** where the airline publishes one: British Airways
(40×30×15), Turkish (40×30×15 / 4 kg), Jet2 (40×30×20), TUI (40×30×20), Qantas (48×34×23),
ANA (40×30×20), AirAsia (40×30×10). The dataset simply has no row.

Three are the reverse — **a personal-item row the airline does not publish at all**: Delta
and Alaska publish no personal-item dimensions, so the bundled rows (46×36×20 and 43×33×15)
are our inference presented as their rule.

Two are 1 cm rounding, both in the permissive direction: **United** (23 cm recorded, 22 cm
published) and **American** (46×36×20 recorded, 45×35×20 published) — both from rounding
9 in and 18×14×8 in upward instead of using the airline's own centimetre figure.

**One is a fare rule, and it reaches published content:** the dataset labels JetBlue's
personal-item row "Blue Basic". JetBlue added a free carry-on to Blue Basic in September
2024. **The two Spirit pages in `lft/content/` repeat that stale claim** and need the
JetBlue line corrected before publishing.

Also uncaptured: linear-sum limits (IndiGo, Scoot, SQ, JAL, ANA, Korean, Air India,
Malaysia, Air NZ all impose one) and fare-level carry-on exclusions (Air Canada Economy
Basic since Jan 2025, Avianca Basic, SWISS/Austrian Economy Basic, SAS Go Light).

## What I did not do

**No dimension was changed.** Not one. `apply-provenance.py` writes `source`,
`verificationMethod` and, where figures were checked, `lastVerified` — plus a `dataIssue`
note on the 37 airlines carrying one.

Every figure here came from a **web search restricted to each airline's own domain**.
**Not one airline domain is reachable from this container** — the environment's network
policy refused allegiantair.com, aerlingus.com, flydubai.com and every other one tried — so
I could not open a single page and read it. Overwriting 15 airlines' published allowances
on that basis is exactly the mistake that put flydubai's numbers on Aer Lingus.

## What this means for the plan

The enforcement index was going to be the citable asset pitched to journalists. **It cannot
be pitched at a 32% error rate.** A travel desk that spot-checks three airlines finds one
wrong.

Fixing the 15 is now the first task in the backlink sequence, ahead of the embed outreach —
and the tool is giving readers wrong answers today, which matters more than any link.
